Historical Perspective on Interest in the
Endocrine System
The significance of the endocrine system in strength and
conditioning gained prominence with the rise of anabolic drug use in
the 1940s and 1950s (7, 58). Fahey and colleagues were the first to
investigate how resistance exercises affected endogenous hormone
concentrations, specifically testosterone, across different sexes,
ages, and training statuses, paving the way for ongoing research
into hormonal responses to resistance exercise and training (23). In
1988, a series of reviews were published from the first symposium
on the physiology of resistance exercise and training at the American
College of Sports Medicine’s annual meeting. As part of that
symposium set of papers, Kraemer (97) overviewed the sparse
number of studies that had been done on the various hormonal
responses to resistance exercise and training. This stimulated a line
of hormonal research that continues today to explore further the
mechanisms behind these acute responses and training adaptations
(46). Now the body of literature consists of thousands of studies on
this topic.

Endocrine Terminology, Functions, and
Mechanisms
Grasping the effects of resistance exercise on hormonal responses
requires a solid understanding of endocrine system terminology.
While the learning curve is significant, becoming familiar with this
language is vital for effectively translating scientific insights into
practical training strategies. Hormones, the chemical messengers
or signal molecules, are synthesized, often stored, and released into
the blood by endocrine glands and certain other cells (figure 4.1,
table 
4.1). 
Neurons 
also 
synthesize, 
store, 
and 
secrete
neurotransmitters, which may perform hormonal functions, a study
known as neuroendocrinology—exploring interactions between
the nervous and endocrine systems. Typically, endocrine glands are
prompted to release hormones by a chemical signal received by
receptors on the gland or by direct neural stimulation. For example,
the adrenal medulla, the inner part of the adrenal gland, releases
the hormone epinephrine in response to sympathetic neural
stimulation from the brain (68, 74, 87, 129). Meanwhile, the outer
layer of the adrenal cortex produces and releases cortisol when
stimulated by adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) from the pituitary
gland (75, 78, 90). Once released, these hormones travel in the
bloodstream to specific receptors located on the surface (for peptide
hormones) or inside the target tissue cells (for steroid and
thyroid hormones) (4, 5, 6, 8, 22, 39), facilitating a targeted and
efficient response.

Understanding Signaling Concepts
Different types of workouts trigger specific, complex cellular
endocrine signaling processes, thus highlighting the importance of
the selection for each of the five acute program variables and then
the total amount of work performed in a workout (70) for the
endocrine response. Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) primarily directs
protein synthesis, but the regulation and expression of DNA affect
many other cellular functions and the synthesis of various molecules
essential for life. Hormonal signaling processes begin when glands
release hormones during, or in anticipation of, a resistance exercise
workout. These hormones then bind to specific receptors in muscle
cells and other cells. This binding initiates a cascade of responses,
generally 
resulting 
in 
altered 
cellular 
metabolism 
or 
DNA
transcription modulation, which can affect protein synthesis. It is
essential to know that every cell and many subcellular organelles
have genomic and non-genomic receptors, and this is where the
hormone signals its message to make proteins. Here is a simplified
breakdown of the signaling process for skeletal muscle:
1. Stimulating the muscle. Resistance exercise activates muscles,
prompting a hormonal response.
2. Hormonal response. Hormones are released and travel to
muscle cells.
3. Hormone–receptor interaction. Hormones bind to specific
receptors in the cell membrane of muscle cells (peptide
hormones) or inside the muscle cells (steroid hormones),
signaling the need for action inside the cell.
4. Intracellular signaling. This binding triggers a series of events
that lead to DNA signaling inside the nucleus.
5. DNA 
translation. 
DNA 
instructions 
are 
transcribed 
to
messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA), which carries the
instructions to the cell’s protein-making machinery (ribosome).
234

6. Protein production. The mRNA instructions are translated into
specific proteins, the building blocks for muscle repair and
growth.
7. Muscle growth and repair. Regular training uses this muscle
development and repair mechanism, which is essential for
developing lean muscle mass and athletic performance.

Functions of the Endocrine System in
Resistance Exercise and Training
The major functions of the endocrine system can be classified into
the following key hormonal actions:
Anabolic responses (to build up). Hormones such as
testosterone, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), and growth
hormone play critical roles in muscle growth and repair.
Permissive actions (to allow). Hormones such as insulin and
thyroid hormones enable critical metabolic and physiological
activities by enhancing nutrient uptake and promoting
essential chemical reactions in muscles, thereby supporting
muscle growth and overall metabolic functions.
Catabolic processes (to break down). For example, cortisol
helps manage energy during stress but can hinder muscle
growth if not properly managed through adequate rest and
recovery. This emphasizes the balance needed in training
regimens.
Types of Hormonal Release Mechanisms
Hormones are typically released into the bloodstream via the classic
endocrine pathway after gland stimulation. Hormones can also
operate through intracrine, autocrine, and paracrine mechanisms,
which do not rely on the bloodstream. Intracrine and autocrine
actions involve hormones either binding to internal receptors within
their producing cell or being released to bind to surface receptors on
the same cell. An example of this is insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-
1) and its splice variant mechano growth factor (MGF), which muscle
fibers produce in response to mechanical stress or interaction with
growth hormones, affecting the muscle cell directly. Paracrine action
occurs when hormones affect adjacent cells, again bypassing the
bloodstream.
236

Binding Proteins
Hormones are often transported in the blood by various binding
proteins (4, 6), which serve as a temporary storage location and
protect the hormones from degradation, thereby extending their
lifetime in the body (called the half-life). It is important to note that
hormones typically exert their effects only when they are released,
and thus are “free,” from these proteins. However, some binding
proteins, such as sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG), which binds
testosterone and estrogen, can initiate cellular responses themselves
by 
activating 
pathways, 
including 
the 
cyclic 
adenosine
monophosphate (cAMP) pathway (29). These interactions are crucial
for understanding endocrine regulation and continue as an area of
study (101).
Target Cells and Tissues
Hormones influence a wide array of cells by interacting with
receptors throughout the body (1, 2, 57, 59, 62, 63). While our focus
is on skeletal muscle tissue, it is important to recognize that
resistance exercise–induced hormone release also affects bone,
other connective tissues, the kidneys, the liver, immune cells, and
neural tissues, including the brain. Interestingly, not only do these
target cells in the different tissues adapt to the exercise stress, but
their receptors also gain a positive adaptive effect with training.

Importance of Recruitment of Muscle
Tissue
Henneman’s size principle shows that as the resistance increases
(i.e., heavier loads), more and larger muscle fibers are recruited to
meet the increased force demand. This principle outlines the
recruitment order of motor units—from smaller, slow-twitch fiber
motor units to larger, fast-twitch fiber motor units. The heavier the
resistance, the greater the number and size of motor units recruited
in a muscle to produce force (77). While anabolic and catabolic
hormones maintain homeostatic balance by interacting with all cells,
stress causes hormonal changes that reflect the body’s need to meet
new metabolic demands and recovery needs. However, only
recruited motor units adapt to the training stimulus and are affected
by the endocrine system’s increase in anabolic hormones, likely due
to the increased activation of more receptors. Interestingly, and of
importance, increased catabolic hormone responses to stress or
distress can affect all tissues even if not recruited for exercise
performance. A catabolic state is highly detrimental for an athlete,
especially when cortisol levels remain chronically elevated. Combined
with the presence of reactive oxygen species (free radicals)
generated by the body’s inflammatory response, this condition
significantly impairs recovery and hampers performance (77).

Muscle as the Target for Hormone
Interactions
Resistance exercise and training leads to muscle remodeling through
a complex network of metabolic and cellular signals. This process
involves muscle fiber damage, an inflammatory response, and the
breakdown of damaged proteins, ultimately synthesizing new
proteins integrated into muscle structures. The inflammatory phase,
which involves the immune system, including T and B cells, is
significantly influenced by hormonal activity, emphasizing the role of
neuroendocrine immunology in understanding how the nervous,
endocrine, and immune systems interact in muscle adaptation (77).
Hormones regulate the balance between protein synthesis and
degradation, playing a critical role in muscle adaptation after
resistance exercise. They stimulate the production of contractile
proteins like actin and myosin, as well as structural proteins such as
titin, which are essential for muscle function. Anabolic hormones,
including insulin, insulin-like growth factors (IGFs), testosterone, and
GH, are particularly vital because they drive tissue growth and
counteract the effects of catabolic hormones like cortisol and
progesterone, which can lead to protein degradation. Furthermore,
hormones like thyroid hormones play a supportive, permissive role,
facilitating the actions of other hormones. The potentially negative
effects of cortisol in recovery, such as inhibiting immune cell activity
and disrupting pathways like the Akt/mechanistic target of
rapamycin 
(mTOR) 
pathway—important 
for 
initiating 
mRNA
translation—are 
also 
notable, 
demonstrating 
the 
complex
interactions between hormones and muscle fibers that influence
muscle function and adaptation to training.

Role of Receptors in Mediating
Hormonal Changes
The effectiveness of hormonal signals depends entirely on the
presence of hormone-specific receptors on or in the target cells. This
ensures that each hormone only affects its intended cells and tissues
(those that express the specific receptors). Such specificity is crucial
for maintaining precise hormonal regulation within the body. Since
many hormones cannot directly cross the cell membrane, they rely
on binding to membrane receptors that cause secondary
messengers to transmit signals internally, including reaching the
DNA in the cell’s nuclei. These receptors, located either in the cell
membrane for polypeptide hormones or within the cytosol for
steroid and thyroid hormones, allow various cells, from muscle fibers
to immune and brain cells, to respond to hormonal signals.
A fundamental principle in endocrinology, often described through
the lock-and-key model (24), explains how a hormone (the key)
must precisely match a receptor (the lock) to trigger a response
(figure 4.2). However, this model oversimplifies the complexity of
hormone–receptor interactions. For example, cross-reactivity
allows receptors to engage with hormones other than their primary
matches, and allosteric binding can modify how a receptor
responds to its primary hormone (such as blocking binding to the
receptor and thus signaling). Additionally, receptors might have
allosteric sites where nonhormonal substances can enhance or
reduce the hormonal response. Some hormones, like GH, might even
need to aggregate into specific forms (multiple hormones bound
together in a “cluster”) to effectively signal through their receptors,
as shown in studies using the tibial line assay (40), indicating that
specific molecular configurations are essential for their activity.

Adaptation Limits and Receptor Sensitivity
When a muscle fiber has reached its capacity for protein
incorporation, or when a chronically elevated concentration of a
hormone 
causes 
overstimulation, 
receptors 
may 
become
desensitized or unresponsive—a condition known as receptor
downregulation; a classic example of this phenomenon is insulin
resistance, which can be caused by chronically elevated circulating
insulin concentrations. The reduced signal from the hormone
following overstimulation signifies a reduced ability of the hormone
to interact with its receptor or downregulation of the number of
receptors, which can effectively stop further cellular responses and
adaptations (37, 122). As such, receptors can adjust their affinity for
binding, and the number of available receptors can vary. These
changes affect cellular adaptation as significantly, if not more, as
changes in hormone concentrations. For example, heavy resistance
overtraining can lead to a downregulation of beta-2 receptors,
affecting their interaction with epinephrine and reducing muscle
contractile force (37). Exploring how receptor characteristics alter in
response to training and affect muscle adaptation is key to
understanding the complex interactions between hormones and
exercise.

Categories of Hormones
Hormones can be classified into three main categories based on their
molecular structure: steroid, peptide (or polypeptide), and amine
hormones.
Steroid Hormone Interactions
Steroid hormones, including cortisol, testosterone, and estradiol
produced by the adrenal cortex and/or gonads, can diffuse across
cell membranes due to their lipophilic (fat-soluble) nature. They
engage with specific receptors inside the cell to form a hormone–
receptor 
complex 
(H-RC). 
This 
complex 
undergoes 
a
conformational change, activates, and pairs with another H-RC. It
then moves to the nucleus, interacts with the DNA, and promotes
the transcription process for specific proteins by “unzipping” DNA
and identifying regulatory elements of the targeted gene (18, 117).
The resulting mRNA is then translated by ribosomes in the
sarcoplasm into the intended proteins. The steroid hormone–induced
protein synthesis process is depicted in figure 4.3.
Testosterone’s influence on muscle cells encompasses more than
its genomic effects. In addition to the primary nuclear receptor, a
membrane receptor for testosterone has also been described in
detail (128). Such a membrane receptor allows testosterone to affect
the cell through rapid and nongenomic interactions (i.e., those that
do not involve DNA transcription in the nuclei) within various other
cellular compartments. These interactions play a crucial role in
muscle adaptation, recovery, and growth by facilitating the
production 
of 
essential 
contractile 
and 
structural 
proteins,
modulating calcium flux in muscle cells, altering membrane fluidity,
and activating key signaling pathways (e.g., the MAPK/ERK
pathway). Such nongenomic actions underscore testosterone’s
multifaceted 
role 
in 
muscle 
development. 
Understanding
testosterone’s genomic and nongenomic effects is vital for a
243

comprehensive grasp of steroid hormones’ impact on muscle biology
(91).
Polypeptide Hormone Interactions
Polypeptide hormones are composed of chains of amino acids and
include hormones such as GH and insulin. Due to their non-lipophilic
nature, polypeptide hormones cannot cross the cell membrane, and,
therefore, their effects are mediated by secondary messengers
within the cell. When these hormones bind to their membrane
receptor, they cause a conformational change that allows the
receptor to transmit the hormonal signal inside the cell. This
transmission initiates a cascade of signaling events that, among
others, can affect metabolic activities, influence DNA transcription, or
kick-start mRNA translation at the ribosome. For example, insulin
activates a pathway that results in glucose transporters (specifically
GLUT4) migrating from the cytosol to the cell membrane, enhancing
the cell’s uptake of glucose (45). This is crucial for managing blood
glucose levels and cellular substrate availability for ATP production.

Amine Hormone Interactions
Amine hormones are indeed synthesized from amino acids, including
those derived from tyrosine (such as epinephrine, norepinephrine,
and dopamine) and tryptophan (such as serotonin), and primarily
exert their effects by binding to membrane receptors. These
hormones typically act through secondary messenger systems,
similar to polypeptide hormones. For example, epinephrine and
norepinephrine interact with G protein-coupled receptors to initiate
signaling cascades involving cAMP or other secondary messengers.

Training-Mediated Hormonal Responses
and Mechanisms
Electrical, chemical, and hormonal signals are transmitted from the
brain, active muscles, and other tissues to various endocrine glands,
preparing the body’s physiological systems for the demands of the
workout (20, 23, 28, 32, 35, 49, 75, 80, 81). Heavy resistance
training over months to years significantly enhances musculature
size, strength, and power, mediated partly through adaptive
hormonal responses (47, 50, 70, 73, 76, 88, 95). These adaptations
arise from increased levels of anabolic hormones such as
testosterone, growth hormone, and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-
1) that acutely rise during exercise and remain elevated after
exercise. Additionally, resistance exercise leads to an upregulation of
hormone receptors in muscle tissues, which increases the muscle’s
sensitivity to these hormones and enhances protein synthesis and
muscle hypertrophy. The structural changes in muscle and neural
adaptations contribute to increased strength and power, including
improvements in the rate of force production and alterations in
muscle fiber recruitment patterns. Resistance training also positively
affects bone density and the strength of connective tissues,
supporting increased muscular strength and overall physical
performance. One critical pathway in muscle protein synthesis
activated by resistance training is the mammalian target of the
rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, which is crucial for muscle growth.
These complex interactions result in adaptive changes in skeletal
muscle and other tissues, enhancing the activation of muscle
functions over time.
Acute hormonal releases reflect the nature and intensity of
physiological stress, such as insulin-related metabolic demands,
prompting adjustments in resting metabolism. These coordinated
hormonal responses are critical for adapting to training programs,
influencing tissue remodeling, and promoting anabolic actions.
248

However, excessive exercise stress may lead to catabolic dominance
due to factors like impaired hormone receptor binding or receptor
downregulation in muscle tissue, highlighting the importance of
appropriate exercise stimulus for beneficial hormonal responses (18,
25, 26, 27, 30, 69, 96, 99, 108).

Interpreting Hormonal Changes in
Peripheral Blood
Understanding exercise effects on the body goes beyond physical
observations; it includes reading studies that monitor biochemical
changes such as hormone fluctuations in blood, muscle, and
surrounding fluids (114). These changes provide insights into the
body’s stress response and adaptation to exercise. However,
interpreting these changes can be complex, because hormone levels
in the blood are just one aspect of a broader hormonal response
that also includes neural and nutritional factors such as being fasted
or not and if proteins and carbohydrates are ingested around a
workout. Increases in blood hormone levels generally indicate
enhanced release from endocrine glands, potentially boosting
hormone–receptor interactions in target tissues. Yet the actual
impact depends on the state and quantity of the receptors—whether
they are primed to bind hormones and initiate crucial processes
involved in protein synthesis, including activating pathways such as
mTOR (11, 13, 14, 66). Conversely, decreases in hormone levels
might suggest increased tissue uptake, faster hormone breakdown,
or reduced secretion.
Several physiological factors, including circadian rhythms, fluid
volume changes, tissue clearance rates, venous pooling, and
interactions with binding proteins, also influence observed circulating
hormone concentrations after exercise. These factors determine the
hormone’s efficacy in interacting with receptors and triggering
cellular responses, underlining the complexity of hormone function
and its role in exercise adaptations.
While increased hormone levels after exercise indicate heightened
glandular activity, they do not directly correlate with muscle gains.
Muscle size and strength can improve without significant changes in
hormone levels by, for example, using heavy 1RM to 3RM zones with
long rest periods of over 3 minutes. Such resistance loads recruit
250

maximal amounts of motor units and enhance receptor binding.
However, such workouts’ relatively low metabolic demands typically
result in only modest, if any, increases in blood concentrations of
testosterone, IGF-1, and growth hormone. Nevertheless, slight
hormonal changes, combined with paracrine and autocrine signaling,
are generally sufficient to effectively signal via receptors and other
molecular pathways within tissues in the neuromuscular system to
stimulate growth and repair. Conversely, a workout that involves
8RM to 10RM resistances, short rest periods of 1 to 2 minutes, and a
higher volume of work produces greater metabolic demands on the
body. The metabolic stress is evidenced by significant increases in
blood lactate and a surge in both anabolic and catabolic hormones.
These hormonal changes signal skeletal muscle receptors effectively,
even with the recruitment of fewer motor units using lighter
resistances. Periodization strategies that incorporate a range of
different resistances over time ensure effective muscle activation
across various workout styles, where hormones help mediate
changes. Such workout design variations reflect the complex nature
of exercise-induced hormonal activation and its contribution to
adaptive changes in the body.
Hormone responses in the blood are tightly linked to the
characteristics of the resistance exercise protocol.

Adaptations in the Endocrine System
From Resistance Training
Resistance training affects muscles and connective tissues and
significantly affects the other physiological systems, including the
endocrine system. This type of training also trains the endocrine
glands, with the gland’s involvement dependent on the demands of
the motor units activated during exercise. The endocrine system’s
adaptations to resistance training include changes in the following:
Hormone synthesis and storage
Hormone transport via binding proteins
Clearance and degradation rates of hormones
Fluid shifts from blood to tissues during exercise
Hormone–receptor binding affinity
The number of tissue receptors
Glandular cell changes
The intensity of signals sent to cell nuclei by hormone–
receptor complexes
Cellular interactions influencing muscle protein production
Hormones are secreted to maintain homeostasis, regulating
physiological functions during both the acute stress of a workout and
chronic stress from long-term training, which activates these
homeostatic mechanisms (17, 33, 34, 41, 49, 56, 125, 126, 130).
These adaptations might lead to subtle changes in resting hormone
levels, demonstrating the dynamic responsiveness of the endocrine
system to physical training.

Primary Anabolic Hormones in Muscle
Development
Key anabolic hormones that drive muscle tissue growth and
remodeling include testosterone, growth hormone, and insulin-like
growth factors (IGFs), with additional roles played by insulin and
thyroid hormones (25, 26, 27, 28, 38, 91).
Testosterone’s Role in Muscle Adaptation
Testosterone, the primary androgen affecting skeletal muscle, and
dihydrotestosterone, which acts mainly on sex-linked tissues, play
crucial roles in the body. While circulating testosterone levels
indicate anabolic activity, the actual binding of testosterone to its
receptor is vital for stimulating muscle growth. Binding to androgen
genomic and non-genomic receptors activates various signaling
pathways that enhance gene expression for muscle hypertrophy and
suppress those involved in protein degradation. Therefore, increased
testosterone levels due to increased motor unit activation indicate
metabolic demands beyond homeostatic conditions and are
associated with enhanced receptor binding (124, 133).
Testosterone also indirectly affects muscle growth by promoting
the release of growth hormone from the pituitary, which influences
muscle protein synthesis. This hormone’s relationship with growth
hormone is either permissive or synergistic in promoting protein
synthesis (103). Furthermore, testosterone affects the nervous
system 
by 
interacting 
with 
neuron 
receptors, 
altering
neurotransmitter amounts, and influencing structural protein
changes within muscle cells (9, 67, 91).
After secretion from the primary production sites, the testes in
men and the ovaries and adrenal cortex in women, testosterone is
carried to target tissues by binding proteins, primarily sex hormone–
binding globulin and albumin. At the target site, testosterone
dissociates from these proteins and crosses the cell membrane to
253

bind to intracellular androgen receptors, directly influencing DNA
transcription. Testosterone also binds to membrane receptors,
enabling rapid intracellular effects like calcium release (21, 131).
Understanding the varied impacts of testosterone and its
interaction with exercise conditions reveals how differential forces on
cell membranes during resistance training or feedback mechanisms
to the brain can alter cellular actions and receptor interactions (18).
Despite increases in testosterone concentrations during high-
intensity aerobic endurance exercise, significant hypertrophy does
not typically occur. The lack of hypertrophy may be due to oxidative
stress, which can lead to reduced type I muscle fiber size to optimize
oxygen 
transport 
distances 
to 
mitochondria. 
High-intensity
endurance training, including intervals performed alone, has been
shown to lead to decreases in type I muscle fibers (88).
Influence of Sex Hormones
Testosterone, while primarily recognized as a male sex hormone,
also plays a significant role in women, who naturally have only about
5% to 10% of the circulating testosterone levels found in men due
to the absence of testes. Research on testosterone responses in
women following resistance exercise has yielded mixed results. Most
studies have not shown a notable acute increase in total
testosterone after workouts; if increases are observed, they are
generally small (15, 23, 51, 56, 81, 134). However, some studies
note these increases specifically in free testosterone, which is not
bound to binding proteins and thus can interact freely with cells
(133).
A study found that younger women experienced a modest but
significant increase in serum testosterone after performing six sets
of squats at a 10RM with 2 minutes of rest between sets at 80% of
the 1 RM (111). Additionally, Vingren and colleagues observed that
highly resistance-trained men and women exhibited acute rises in
free testosterone following heavy resistance exercises. However, the
values in women were significantly lower than those in men (133).
254

While women have lower baseline testosterone levels, exercise can
still influence their hormone levels.
Moreover, individual variations exist among women, with some
producing higher concentrations of adrenal androgens. A study
comparing active women with inactive controls found differences in
baseline testosterone levels, indicating that regular exercise might
influence these levels (15). Despite these findings, other research
has not consistently demonstrated changes in serum testosterone
concentrations in women with training (23, 51, 56, 81, 134),
highlighting the complexity of hormonal responses to resistance
exercise in women.
While testosterone levels in women are lower, the dynamic
interaction between testosterone, SHBG, DHT, and androgen
receptors facilitates significant responses to resistance exercise.
These interactions can include muscle mass and strength increases,
essential for physical health and metabolic function. The response of
androgen receptors to even small increases in testosterone and the
compensatory role of other androgens like DHT emphasize the
complex but critical nature of androgens in female physiology and
resistance exercise.
An interplay between free and total testosterone is critical for
optimizing exercise outcomes. Free testosterone, which represents
only 0.5% to 2% of total testosterone, is vital for direct interaction
with muscle tissues. Younger men generally exhibit higher
postexercise increases in free testosterone compared to older men,
indicating a greater possibility for muscle tissue interaction (82).
According to the free hormone hypothesis, the free, unbound
hormone interacts with target tissues. However, bound hormones
may also enhance hormone delivery to these tissues, potentially
benefiting younger individuals more significantly (19). Due to
women’s relatively low total testosterone levels, changes in
hormone-binding proteins could substantially regulate hormone
activity, suggesting that these binding proteins might act as bioactive
hormones (120).

Exercise-Induced Responses
Men’s testosterone levels are generally highest in the morning and
gradually decrease throughout the day. However, this does not mean
that morning workouts are more effective than afternoon workouts.
The effectiveness of exercise depends more on the availability of
hormone receptors in both men and women, which is not directly
linked to the body’s circadian rhythm. Exercise can trigger increases
any time, with the overall response related to intensity, volume, and
rest periods between sets (17, 23, 44, 48, 81, 134). However, the
magnitude of the testosterone’s response is, as with all hormones,
dependent on where blood is sampled in the hormone’s circadian
cycle (e.g., testosterone is higher in the morning than in the late
afternoon), and thus, it is important to understand where a measure
of a hormone was made and if the study has a resting control for
the time of day to determine the magnitude above resting levels
accurately. Its circadian cycle is important for the interpretation of
the results of a study. Most studies have used the morning time
frames.
Testosterone dynamics in young males, particularly those under
18, are influenced significantly by puberty, affecting their responses
to exercise. Prepubescent boys produce minimal testosterone, which
limits exercise-induced hypertrophy. Adolescent males can enhance
serum testosterone levels through exercises that engage large
muscle groups like squats and deadlifts, using heavy resistance of
85% to 95% of their 1RM, and maintaining high exercise volume
with short rest intervals between sets, but the levels in women were
dramatically lower (figure 4.5; 133). It took two years of training in
young weightlifters (14-17 years) before an exercise-induced
testosterone effect was observed. While resistance training may not
raise resting testosterone levels, repeated exposure to resistance
training could help younger boys develop their hypothalamic-
pituitary-gonadal axis, enabling a more robust testosterone response
to acute resistance exercise (35).

Impact on Androgen Receptors
Research continues to expand our understanding of how
testosterone responds to resistance training, revealing that
testosterone levels dynamically respond to exercise demands,

influencing receptor activity and adaptations in different tissues like

expectations that testosterone consistently increases in resting
states may be outdated; however, exercise-induced testosterone
levels are expected to rise due to enhanced functional capacity and
workload during training sessions. These hormonal changes may be
linked to neural adaptations that enhance performance. Häkkinen
and colleagues (49) noted little change in muscle fiber size despite
the increases in testosterone, follicle-stimulating hormone, and
luteinizing hormone. Such data demonstrate that the improvements
were more likely driven by neural factors augmented by hormonal
changes rather than significant muscle growth.
Over time, factors such as training duration and experience play
critical roles in hormonal adjustments. For instance, a notable study
by Häkkinen and colleagues (49) observed small but significant
increases in resting serum testosterone levels over two years among
elite weightlifters, alongside increases in follicle-stimulating hormone
and luteinizing hormone, which are crucial in regulating testosterone
production.
Testosterone also supports the development of the nervous
system and enhances neural adaptations necessary for strength
gains, particularly in highly trained athletes (51, 52). Additionally,
training sessions that acutely elevate testosterone levels have been
linked to more remarkable resistance training adaptations (53, 119).
The effects of resistance training on androgen receptors—
testosterone’s binding sites—are emerging as an area of significant
interest, with studies showing both increases and decreases in
androgen receptor content and DNA binding, depending on the
exercise protocol and timing of tissue sampling (12, 93, 118, 132,
133, 136). For example, Cardaci and colleagues noted that androgen
receptor-DNA binding increased only after high-intensity total body
resistance exercises and not after low-intensity exercises (12).

boost muscle androgen receptor content, thereby enhancing

because only small increases in the exercise-induced response have
been noted. Yet the androgen receptors in trained women rapidly
respond to the available testosterone after exercise (111, 133).

Growth Hormone
Growth hormone (GH) is produced in the pituitary gland by
somatotroph cells, divided into two types based on their secretion
patterns and the forms of GH. Band 1 somatotrophs generate the
smaller, newly synthesized 22 kD molecular weight isoform of GH, a
191 amino acid polypeptide. Conversely, band 2 somatotrophs
produce aggregates of these smaller isoforms (e.g., two 22 kD units
combining to form a 44 kD molecule), which are considered older
and are potential GH storage forms (61). These aggregates can be
released into the bloodstream, interacting with specific tissue
receptors. For instance, aggregated GH binds to receptors in the
tibial growth plate to stimulate bone growth. These different
molecular weight variants target various tissues, including bone,
muscle, liver, and immune cells, influencing glucose use, protein
synthesis, lipolysis, collagen synthesis, and enhancing immune cell
function.
The presence of different GH isoforms in the blood can only be
distinguished through various assays, with most studies focusing on
the smaller 22 kD isoform using immunoassays. This focus has
shaped much of our current understanding of GH’s role and function
in the body, but the aggregated forms of GH might have greater
biological potency and importance (61, 91). Figure 4.6 outlines the
basic feedback mechanisms regulating the production of GH
isoforms, highlighting the complex control the pituitary gland
exercises over GH secretion.

The body’s response to GH varies with resistance exercise, and
understanding these responses is crucial for optimizing training
outcomes. Initially observed in studies from the late 1970s to early
1990s, resistance training can acutely elevate GH levels in the blood,
particularly through exercises involving large muscle groups and high
intensity (100, 123, 130). Research has shown that the type of
workout—especially the intensity, volume, and rest periods—
significantly affects acute GH responses (75, 83). Short rest periods
combined with moderate resistance tend to produce higher GH
spikes compared to longer rest periods.
While resting concentrations are not typically affected by training,
the ability to do more total work or tolerate greater increases in H+
ions and resultant pH decreases allows for greater peak GH
concentrations in recovery. While general responses are similar to
those in men, subtle differences exist due to factors such as the
menstrual cycle and hormonal contraception, affecting GH levels and
their response to exercise. Yet the impact on training appears
minimal (79, 81, 85).
Resistance training affects the various molecular or aggregate
forms of GH, with evidence showing that high-intensity workouts
lead to increased production of the 22 kD GH isoform. However,
research using bioassays suggests that resistance training might
reduce the release of larger GH aggregates, indicating a shift toward
more newly synthesized GH and less storage in aggregates (60, 72,
104). This adaptation might reflect an evolved response in the
anterior pituitary gland, particularly in highly trained individuals.

Insulin-Like Growth Factors and Binding
Proteins
IGFs, primarily IGF-1 and IGF-2, are small polypeptides crucial for
various anabolic processes in the body, including cellular growth,
development, and metabolism. These growth factors are structurally
similar to insulin and originate from larger precursor molecules
modified posttranslationally to become active. IGF-1, the most
researched, is predominantly synthesized in the liver in response to
stimulation by 22 kD GH (16, 22, 27, 91). Besides GH, IGF synthesis
is also influenced by thyroid hormones and testosterone (137, 138,
139, 140).
IGFs perform several functions, including enhancing protein
synthesis, increasing amino acid transport across cell membranes,
and stimulating growth in tissues such as muscle and bone. In the
bloodstream, IGFs are mostly bound to proteins, with only about 2%
in the free (unbound) form; same as for other hormones, the
binding proteins regulate IGF activity and availability. These binding
proteins are critical in modulating the growth-promoting actions of
the IGFs (91). Additionally, IGFs stimulate the secretion of their
binding proteins within muscle cells, influencing cellular sensitivity to
IGFs (105). IGF binding proteins (IGFBPs) are a group of proteins
that regulate the bioavailability and activity of IGFs. There are six
main types of IGFBPs (IGFBP-1 to IGFBP-6) found in humans, each
with distinct binding affinities, tissue distributions, and biological
functions. IGFBPs can inhibit or enhance the action of IGFs by doing
the following:
1. Binding IGFs with high affinity, which prevents their interaction
with IGF receptors and modulates their half-life, transport, and
tissue localization
2. Regulating IGF turnover and clearance in the circulation
263

3. Interacting with cellular components, influencing various
cellular processes independently of IGFs
IGFBPs are involved in numerous physiological processes,
including growth regulation, cell proliferation, and differentiation.

IGF Responses to Exercise
The role of IGFs extends to exercise response, where resistance
exercise particularly influences their production and activity.
Mechanical stress from resistance exercise triggers muscle fibers to
produce IGF-1 locally, which then acts to stimulate muscle growth
and repair. This local production is often more critical than the
circulating levels of IGF-1, which may not consistently change with
resistance exercise (109, 113). The response of IGF-1 to heavy
resistance exercise can vary, influenced by the initial levels of IGF-1
before training and the response of GH. Studies indicate that lower
baseline IGF-1 levels typically lead to increases after exercise,
whereas higher baseline levels may result in no change or even
decreases (W.J. Kraemer, unpublished data). Nutritional status,
including caloric intake and restriction, also significantly modulates
resting and exercise-induced IGF-1 concentrations (55, 92). In
women, resistance training has increased total IGF-1 levels and
decreased IGF-binding protein-1 during workouts, suggesting
exercise-specific adaptations in the IGF system (43). The IGF
superfamily’s complex response patterns to training involve
mechanisms such as IGF type, release, transport, and receptor
interactions, along with interactions with other anabolic hormones
targeting similar outcomes such as protein synthesis. However,
highly trained men and women both showed increases in IGF-1
concentrations, but women demonstrated values higher than men at
all time points. In these trained men and women, IGFBP-1 and 2
showed increases with exercise with no sex-dependent differences,
yet IGFBP-3 concentrations were higher in women at all time points
with no exercise-induced changes (54).

Training Adaptations and IGF and

The full scope of training-induced IGF-1 adaptations across different
tissues continues to be a significant area for research (91, 112).
Training adaptations from resistance exercise influence IGF levels
and their interactions at the cellular level. Resistance training
enhances the activation and expression of IGF-1 receptors, boosting
the signaling pathways essential for cell growth and survival.
However, the full scope of how different training regimens affect IGF
levels, particularly the balance between local (muscle) and systemic
(circulating) IGF, remains a rich area for research. As noted in a
study by Hatfield and colleagues, in highly resistance-trained men
and women (i.e., squat 1RM: 2.1 ± 0.4 and 1.7 ± 0.6 × body mass,
respectively), training affects the resting and acute responses of

in 
their 
response 
patterns 
for

understand the complex dynamics of IGFs in response to resistance
training, including the specific effects of exercise on different forms
of IGF and their binding proteins. This research will help optimize
training strategies and potentially inform therapeutic interventions
targeting muscle health and performance (102, 106).

Adrenal Gland, Cortisol, and Its Role in
Resistance Exercise
The adrenal gland is integral to the body’s adaptation to stress,
particularly during and after exercise. Structurally, it consists of two
main components: the medulla and the cortex, each responding to
stress in distinct ways. The adrenal medulla, the inner core of the
gland, is directly stimulated by the autonomic nervous system during
acute stress. It rapidly secretes catecholamines such as epinephrine
and norepinephrine, alongside dopamine and specific enkephalin-
containing polypeptides like peptide F, which are implicated in
immediate stress responses and immune enhancement (129). The
adrenal cortex is stimulated by adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)
from the anterior pituitary gland to produce and release cortisol.
Cortisol has critical roles in modulating inflammation and managing
metabolic responses to stress. As such, cortisol is regulated on a
systemic level by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and
locally within tissues by enzymes such as 11β-hydroxy- steroid
dehydrogenase (11β-HSD) to maintain glucocorticoid balance (25,
27, 99). Cortisol is crucial for sustaining energy during prolonged
stress by facilitating glucose availability through selective glucose
sparing and mobilization, gluconeogenesis, and fat and protein
catabolism. However, prolonged elevation of cortisol levels—if not
returned to baseline within 24 hours—can adversely affect the body,
because cortisol in this context is considered catabolic and anti-
anabolic. Sustained high cortisol can impair immune function, affect
fat metabolism and deposition, reduce protein synthesis, increase
protein degradation, and interfere with anabolic signaling pathways,
thereby promoting a catabolic state that can undermine muscle
repair and growth (75, 87, 89, 92, 129). Interference in anabolic
signaling is partly due to the cortisol significantly contributing to
immune suppression observed after intense exercise stress. Cortisol
is crucial in “inactivating” immune cell functions during recovery
267

phases, vital for post-exercise muscle remodeling and overall
recovery (30).
Cortisol is critical in energy balance, immune regulation, and
vascular integrity within skeletal muscle. It aids in increasing energy
availability and modulating immune activity during exercise. After
exercise, cortisol helps reduce inflammation and muscle damage, but
sensitivity to cortisol decreases within 24 hours to protect against
prolonged exposure. Athletes show a refined capability to convert
cortisol to cortisone, protecting tissues during exercise stress.
However, overtraining can impair this conversion process, potentially
inhibiting anabolic activities like muscle growth by reducing IGF-I
synthesis (27, 30, 91).
Despite its reputation as a catabolic hormone, cortisol is essential
for carbohydrate metabolism, particularly when muscle glycogen
stores are low (25, 27, 99). It plays a role in converting amino acids
to carbohydrates, increasing proteolytic enzymes, inhibiting
protein synthesis, and suppressing glycogenesis and immune
function (30, 91). Cortisol levels increase in response to resistance
training, particularly with high intensity or volume involving large
muscle masses. The acute hormonal response prepares the body for
future physical demands and aids recovery.
The resistance exercise cortisol response differs some between
men and women. Men generally experience higher increases in
cortisol from certain types of training protocols, such as high-volume
workouts, whereas women rapidly adjust their receptor sensitivity to
hormones after exercise. Such data indicate a sex-specific adaptation
to training stress (133). Emerging research shows that resistance
exercise can affect glucocorticoid receptor concentrations differently
across sexes, indicating potential sex differences in hormonal
response to training. These findings underscore the complex
interaction between cortisol and its receptors in regulating muscle
adaptation and recovery (31).

Catecholamines and Their Role in
Resistance Exercise and Training
Catecholamines, 
primarily 
epinephrine 
but 
also 
including
norepinephrine and dopamine, are secreted by the adrenal medulla
and play a crucial role in enhancing strength and power during
resistance training. These hormones facilitate the fight-or-flight
response by acting as central motor stimulators and peripheral
vascular dilators, enhancing enzyme activity and calcium release in
muscles (89). Notably, higher catecholamine release before and
during intense resistance sessions has been linked to sustained force
output, emphasizing their importance in maintaining performance
(32).
Physiological Functions of Catecholamines
Catecholamines enhance resistance training outcomes by doing the
following:
Increasing force production through central mechanisms and
boosting metabolic enzyme activity
Accelerating muscle contraction rates
Raising blood pressure
Enhancing energy availability and muscle blood flow through
vasodilation
Augmenting the secretion rates of other anabolic hormones
such as testosterone
Acute and Training-Induced Responses
The acute response to high-intensity, short-rest resistance exercises
typical in bodybuilding—characterized by increased levels of
norepinephrine, epinephrine, and dopamine during the session and
brief recovery periods—illustrates catecholamines’ role in meeting
the physical demands of such protocols (86). Epinephrine levels also
correlate with lactate concentrations, reflecting its involvement in
270

metabolic stress responses. Importantly, adrenal responses to stress
are critical until the stressor is removed, with endogenous opioid
peptides like proenkephalins from the adrenal medulla playing roles
in immune system modulation crucial for recovery (129). Continuous
high stress without varied training can lead to delayed recovery and,
eventually, adrenal exhaustion, reducing the adrenal medulla’s ability
to secrete catecholamines effectively.
Training Adaptations and Long-Term
Impacts
Heavy resistance training enhances an athlete’s capacity to secrete
more epinephrine during maximal efforts (87). Consistent training
may decrease epinephrine responses to single sessions, such as a
bench press workout (44). This adaptation suggests that regular
training can modulate catecholamine responses, reducing their surge
during familiar tasks to maintain a more balanced hormonal
environment conducive to ongoing strength gains and recovery.
Understanding the role of catecholamines in resistance training helps
in designing programs that optimize hormonal responses to enhance
strength, 
hypertrophy, 
and 
overall 
athletic 
performance.
Catecholamines’ ability to interact with other hormones underscores
their pivotal position in the endocrine response to resistance
exercise, highlighting the importance of managing exercise intensity,
volume, and recovery to maximize beneficial hormonal interactions.
Training protocols must be varied to allow the adrenal
gland to engage in recovery processes and to prevent
the secondary responses of cortisol, which can have
negative effects on the immune system and protein
structures.

How Can Athletes Manipulate the
Endocrine System With Resistance
Exercise?
General Concepts
The more muscle fibers recruited for an exercise, the greater the extent of
the potential remodeling process in the whole muscle.
Only muscle fibers activated by resistance training are subject to adaptation,
including hormonal adaptations to stress.
To Increase Serum Testosterone Concentrations
Serum testosterone concentrations have been shown to increase acutely by using
these methods independently or in various combinations:
Large muscle group exercises (e.g., deadlift, power clean, squat)
Heavy resistance (85% to 95% 1RM)
Moderate to high volume of exercise, achieved with multiple sets or multiple
exercises
Short rest intervals (30-60 s)
To Increase 22 kD Growth Hormone Concentrations
Growth hormone levels have been shown to increase acutely with use of either of
these methods or both in combination:
Use workouts with higher lactate concentrations and associated acid–base
disruptions; that is, use high intensity (10RM, or heavy resistance) with three
sets of each exercise (high total work) and short (1 min) rest periods.
Supplement diet with carbohydrates and protein before and after workouts.
To Optimize Responses of Adrenal Hormones
Use high volume, large muscle groups, and short rest periods to expose the
body to adrenergic stress.
Be careful to vary the training protocol and the rest period length from short
to long over time.
Provide days of complete rest, and use lower-volume workouts to allow the
adrenal gland to engage in recovery processes to reduce stress on the
adrenal medulla so as not to experience adrenergic exhaustion and to reduce
stress on the adrenal cortex and prevent chronic cortisol secretions from the
272

adrenal cortex. This way, the stress of the exercises will not result in a
nonfunctional overreaching or overtraining.

Other Hormonal Considerations in
Resistance Training
A variety of hormones contribute to normal bodily functions and
adaptively respond to resistance training, including insulin, thyroid
hormones, and beta-endorphin. These hormones play roles in
growth, repair, pain relief, and managing exercise stress, though
research on their specific adaptations to resistance training is limited
(15, 26, 27, 28, 51, 64, 71, 75, 82, 98). For healthy individuals,
significant chronic changes in the resting concentrations of insulin
and thyroid hormones are generally not observed due to tight
homeostatic control. However, resistance training has been noted to
improve insulin sensitivity, possibly reflecting only the acute effects
of recent exercise sessions (10).
Thyroid Hormones
Thyroid hormones, particularly triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine
(T4), play a significant role in both acute and chronic responses to
resistance exercise and training. Immediately following resistance
exercise, T3 levels generally remain unchanged, while T4 levels
might spike briefly and then decrease during subsequent rest
periods (65, 107). This acute response suggests a temporary
adjustment to meet the immediate metabolic demands of exercise.
In the context of long-term resistance training, transient
reductions in T3 and T4 levels can occur, which typically normalize
after several months (3, 116). These fluctuations indicate an initial
adaptation phase in which the thyroid axis adjusts to the increased
demands of regular resistance exercise.
Thyroid hormones are crucial for metabolic control and augment
other hormonal responses, underscoring their importance in
physiological adaptations to training. They support the energy
demands of resistance training by regulating protein synthesis and
mitochondrial activity, contributing to muscle repair and growth.
274

Specifically, T3 enhances protein synthesis by increasing the
transcription of genes involved in muscle hypertrophy and repair.
Additionally, T4’s conversion to T3 in peripheral tissues ensures a
steady supply of active hormones to meet ongoing metabolic needs.
Thyroid hormones also help regulate lipolysis and glucose
metabolism, which are essential for providing the necessary energy
substrates during and after resistance exercise. By modulating these
processes, thyroid hormones help maintain energy balance and
facilitate recovery. Furthermore, thyroid hormones interact with
other endocrine responses during resistance training. For example,
they can enhance the action of GH and IGF-1, both of which are
vital for muscle growth and repair. This synergistic effect amplifies
the overall anabolic environment, promoting more effective muscle
adaptation and strength gains. Thus, thyroid hormones are integral
to immediate and long-term resistance exercise adaptations. They
ensure metabolic homeostasis, support energy production, enhance
protein synthesis, and interact with other hormones to facilitate
muscle growth and repair. Understanding their role highlights the
complex 
hormonal 
interplay 
required 
for 
effective 
training
adaptations and overall metabolic regulation.
Insulin
The insulin response to resistance exercise and training is crucial to
metabolic 
regulation 
and 
muscle 
adaptation. 
Physiologically,
resistance exercise induces an acute increase in insulin sensitivity,
which enhances glucose uptake into skeletal muscle, facilitating
glycogen synthesis and promoting anabolic processes essential for
muscle repair and growth (36, 51). This response is attributed to
increased GLUT4 expression on the cell surface, driven by both
contraction-mediated and insulin-mediated pathways (101). Regular
resistance training amplifies this effect, improving overall short- and
long-term insulin sensitivity. Such data are particularly significant for
metabolic health, because enhanced insulin sensitivity associated
with regular resistance training can contribute to better glycemic
275

control and reduced risk of type 2 diabetes (96, 107). Furthermore,
the increased muscle mass from long-term resistance training
enhances basal metabolic rate and glucose disposal, stabilizing
insulin levels and improving metabolic efficiency (115, 119). Thus,
the insulin response to resistance exercise supports immediate
energy needs and recovery after exercise and contributes to long-
term metabolic health and disease prevention.

Conclusion
The endocrine system’s interaction with the nervous, immune, and
musculoskeletal systems forms a complex network crucial for
optimizing health and athletic performance. Hormones coordinate a
wide range of cellular activities, influencing muscle synthesis,
recovery, and overall physiological well-being. The dynamic balance
between anabolic hormones, which promote muscle growth, and
catabolic hormones, responsible for muscle breakdown and energy
mobilization is vital for responding effectively to resistance training.
Strength 
and 
conditioning 
professionals 
need 
a 
deep
understanding of the endocrine system’s functions to design
effective training programs that maximize performance and minimize
injury risks. This knowledge enables the manipulation of hormonal
responses to both acute exercise and chronic training adaptations,
enhancing recovery and preventing overtraining. The subtle interplay
among various bodily systems and hormones has significant
implications beyond athletic performance, affecting conditions such
as metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular health.
By leveraging insights from the endocrine system, coaches and
athletes can achieve peak performance and support long-term
health.
This overview highlights the sophisticated yet orderly system that
mediates the body’s responses to resistance exercise, emphasizing
the critical role of the endocrine system in sport science and overall
health management. Understanding these complex interactions is
fundamental for strength and conditioning professionals and athletes
aiming to optimize training outcomes and ensure sustained athletic
success and well-being.
